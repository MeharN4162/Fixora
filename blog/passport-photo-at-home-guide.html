How to Take a Passport Photo at Home (No Studio Needed)

Passport and visa photos have some of the strictest formatting rules of any document photo, which is exactly why pharmacies and photo studios charge $15–20 for what's essentially a five-minute setup. If you have a phone camera, a plain wall, and some daylight, you can get an acceptable photo at home — you just need to hit the same technical requirements a studio would. Here's how to do each part correctly, and where a home photo can go wrong.

The Background

Every passport photo standard — US, UK, EU, India, and most others — requires a plain, light-colored background with no texture, no pattern, and no shadow variation. A white or off-white wall is the easiest option most people already have. Avoid:

  • Textured walls (brick, wood paneling, wallpaper with a visible pattern)
  • Doors, light switches, or picture frames creeping into the frame edges
  • A colored wall — most countries specifically require white or off-white, not cream, blue, or grey

Stand at least 3 feet in front of the wall, not against it. This gap is what prevents the next problem: shadows.

Lighting

Lighting is where most home attempts fail. The two things reviewers look for are an evenly lit face and a shadow-free background — both are solved with the same trick.

  • Face a window during the day. Stand so soft daylight falls evenly on your face, rather than to the side (which creates a shadow across half your face) or behind you (which silhouettes you).
  • Never use your camera's flash. Direct flash creates a harsh shadow behind you on the wall and can cause a shiny glare on your skin or glasses.
  • Have someone else take the photo. If you stand a few feet from the wall and someone else holds the camera a few feet in front of you, your own body won't cast a shadow onto the background — this alone fixes most "shadow behind the head" rejections.

Pose and Expression

The pose requirements are nearly universal across countries:

  • Neutral expression — mouth closed, no smiling, both eyes open and looking directly at the camera
  • Head facing forward, not tilted or turned — think "mugshot," not "portrait"
  • Ears don't need to be visible in most countries' current rules, but hair should not cover your eyes or face
  • If you normally wear glasses, check your specific country's current rule first — many countries (including the US) now require glasses to be removed entirely to avoid glare and frame obstruction, while a few still allow them if there's no reflection
Tip: Take 10–15 shots in a row with slightly different head positions and expressions. It's far easier to pick the best one afterward than to nail it on the first try.

What Not to Do

A handful of mistakes account for most home-photo rejections:

  • No filters or beautification apps. Skin-smoothing, slimming, or auto-enhance filters can count as photo manipulation and get an application rejected outright.
  • No other people or objects in frame — including pets, furniture backs, or a second person's shoulder at the edge.
  • No shadows on the background, even faint ones — this is the single most common reason home photos get sent back.
  • No colored or patterned clothing that blends into the background — plain contrasting clothing photographs more reliably.

FAQ

Will a home photo definitely be accepted? Not guaranteed. The Passport Photo Maker tool correctly handles cropping and sizing, but it can't verify your background, lighting, expression, or glasses against your specific country's current rules — that check happens at the passport office or during online submission. If you're on a tight renewal deadline, it's worth double-checking your country's official requirements page before relying solely on a home photo.

Can I use my phone's front camera? Yes, as long as it's high enough resolution (most phones from the last several years are fine) and you're not using a beauty filter or portrait mode, which can blur the background unevenly.

Do I need a tripod? No, but having someone else hold the phone steady a few feet away, rather than using a selfie at arm's length, gives a straighter, more evenly lit result and avoids the wide-angle distortion selfie cameras can introduce.

What if my country isn't in the preset list? Use whichever preset is closest in aspect ratio and dimensions, or check your government's photo specification page for the exact millimeter or pixel size — many countries share the same 35×45mm or 2×2 inch standard.
