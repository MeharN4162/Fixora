Why Time Zones Are So Confusing (And How UTC Fixes It)

Time zones look like they should be simple geography — divide the globe into 24 slices — but political decisions, daylight saving, and inconsistent transition dates make real-world time zone math genuinely tricky.

Why Some Offsets Aren't Whole Hours

Time zone boundaries are political choices, not purely geographic ones. India (UTC+5:30) and Nepal (UTC+5:45) are well-known examples of countries that chose an offset better centered on their territory relative to solar noon, or reflecting a historical preference, rather than sticking to the whole-hour grid most zones follow.

Daylight Saving Time and Scheduling

Not every country observes daylight saving time, and among those that do, the transition dates aren't synchronized globally. This means the offset between two specific cities can genuinely change twice a year, and can shift on different calendar dates in each location — a meeting time correct in early March can be off by an hour in early April if only one side has adjusted its clocks so far.

Tip: For any recurring meeting spanning time zones with different daylight saving rules, double-check the time around each region's spring/fall transition dates specifically — that's when scheduling mistakes happen most often.

Why UTC Is the Universal Reference

UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) never observes daylight saving and isn't tied to any single country's political decisions, which gives it a stable, unchanging reference point that every other time zone can define itself relative to. Using any one country's local time as the reference instead would drag that country's own daylight saving complications into every other calculation built on top of it.

Finding an Overlapping Meeting Time

Convert each location's normal working hours into a single common reference (UTC works well for this), then look for the window where all locations fall within a reasonable working range. Between zones with a large offset, that overlap can be small or nonexistent — which is exactly why globally distributed teams often end up rotating who takes an inconvenient meeting time.
