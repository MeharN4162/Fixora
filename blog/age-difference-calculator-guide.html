"How much older is one person than another" seems like a simple subtraction, but a genuinely accurate answer — one that matches how a person would count it on a calendar — takes a bit more care than dividing total days by 365.

Why Calendar Math Beats Dividing by 365

A quick shortcut for age gaps is dividing the total number of days between two dates by 365. That's close, but it's not exact — leap years mean some years actually have 366 days, so the shortcut slowly drifts off the true calendar answer the longer the span. The accurate approach instead counts full calendar years, then full calendar months within whatever's left, then finally whatever days remain — exactly how you'd count it by hand on a calendar, checking off each birthday month by month.

How Borrowing a Month Works in the Calculation

This calendar-based method mirrors long subtraction with "borrowing." If the day-of-month for the later date is smaller than the day-of-month for the earlier date (say, comparing March 5 to a starting date of January 20), you can't simply subtract days directly — instead, you borrow a full month from the months column, converting it into however many days that previous month actually had (28, 29, 30, or 31, depending on the specific month), then complete the days subtraction. This is exactly the same borrowing logic as subtracting 52 from 71 in base-10 arithmetic, just applied to a calendar instead of a place-value system.

Tip: If you only need the age gap in one unit (just months, or just total days) rather than a full years/months/days breakdown, remember the total-days figure is exact and useful on its own — it's the "divide by 365 to estimate years" shortcut specifically that introduces drift, not total day counting itself.

The Leap Day Birthday Problem

Someone born on February 29 presents a genuine ambiguity: what is their "birthday" in a year where February only has 28 days? There's no single universally agreed answer — some conventions treat it as March 1, others as February 28 — but rolling forward to March 1 in a non-leap year is a common, defensible choice, since it's the next actual calendar date after where February 29 would have fallen.

Why the Order You Enter Dates In Doesn't Matter

A well-built age difference calculator doesn't care which date you type first — it compares the two dates internally, identifies which one is earlier, and calculates the gap from there. This matters in practice because you shouldn't have to remember to always enter the older date first; the calculation should just work regardless of input order, and a good tool handles that automatically rather than producing a negative or nonsensical result if you happen to enter them "backwards."
