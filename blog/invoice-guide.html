How to Write an Invoice That Gets You Paid Faster

A vague or disorganized invoice is one of the most common reasons payment gets delayed — not because the client doesn't want to pay, but because something about the invoice made it easy to set aside. A clear, complete invoice removes every excuse to wait.

What Every Invoice Needs

  • A unique invoice number: makes it referenceable in conversation and easy to track for both sides.
  • Invoice date and due date: removes any ambiguity about when payment is expected.
  • Your business info: name, address, and contact details — and a tax ID if you have one.
  • Client's info: the name and billing details of whoever owes the payment.
  • A clear total, broken down: subtotal, any discount, tax, and the final amount due.

Itemizing Clearly

Break work down into distinct line items rather than one lump total — "Website redesign: $2,000" invites more questions than "Homepage design (10 hrs): $1,000" plus "Contact page build (5 hrs): $500" plus "Revisions (5 hrs): $500." Specificity reduces back-and-forth and makes it obvious the amount is fair.

Tip: If you're billing hourly, include both the hours and the rate per line item, not just the total — it lets a client verify the math themselves instead of having to ask.

Setting Payment Terms

Payment terms state how long a client has to pay after receiving the invoice:

  • Due on receipt: payment expected immediately — common for small jobs or new clients.
  • Net 15: payment due within 15 days — a reasonable middle ground.
  • Net 30: payment due within 30 days — the most common default in many industries, especially with established businesses.

Shorter terms generally mean faster cash flow but can strain a new client relationship; longer terms are more standard for larger companies with slower internal payment processes. State the terms clearly on every invoice — don't assume the client remembers a verbal agreement.

Following Up on Late Payments

Send a brief, polite reminder as soon as the due date passes — plenty of late payments are simple oversights, not disputes. If the invoice stays unpaid past a second reminder, follow up in writing with the specific amount, original due date, and any late fee stated in your terms. Keeping every invoice numbered and dated from the start makes this whole process much easier to manage at scale.

FAQ

What's the difference between Net 15 and Net 30 payment terms? Both describe how many days a client has to pay after the invoice date — Net 15 means payment is due within 15 days, Net 30 within 30 days. Net 30 is the more common default in many industries, but Net 15 (or even "due on receipt") is reasonable for smaller jobs, new clients without an established payment history, or freelancers who need faster cash flow.

Do I need to charge sales tax on my invoice? It depends entirely on your location, the client's location, and what you're selling — many services are exempt from sales tax in some regions while physical goods are not, and the rules vary significantly between states and countries. This isn't something a generic guide can answer definitively; check with a local accountant or your region's tax authority for your specific situation.

What should I do if a client doesn't pay on time? Start with a polite, direct reminder shortly after the due date passes — clients sometimes simply lose track of an invoice. If that doesn't work, follow up with a firmer written notice referencing the original due date and any late fee stated in your terms. For invoices that stay unpaid well past the deadline, options escalate to formal demand letters, collections agencies, or small claims court, depending on the amount involved.

Do invoices need to be sequentially numbered? It's strongly recommended, and required in some jurisdictions for tax and accounting purposes. A consistent numbering scheme (like INV-0001, INV-0002) makes it easy to track which invoices are outstanding, reference a specific invoice in conversation with a client, and keep clean records for tax filing.
