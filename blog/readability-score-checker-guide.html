What the Flesch Reading Ease Score Actually Measures

Readability scores can feel like a black box — you paste in text and a number comes out — but the underlying formula is simple enough to reason about directly, and understanding it makes the score far more actionable than treating it as a mysterious grade.

Two Inputs, One Formula

The Flesch Reading Ease formula boils a whole piece of writing down to just two averages: the average number of words per sentence, and the average number of syllables per word. The formula is 206.835 minus 1.015 times words-per-sentence, minus 84.6 times syllables-per-word. That's it — no vocabulary lists, no grammar analysis, no checking whether a word is "hard" in any semantic sense. It's a purely structural measure of sentence and word length.

Why Sentence Length Dominates the Score

Because the words-per-sentence term is multiplied by a smaller coefficient than syllables-per-word, it might look like syllables matter more — but in practice, sentence length swings much further in real writing. A sentence can easily run from 8 words to 40+, while average syllables per word for most English text stays in a narrower band (usually 1.3 to 1.8). That means breaking up long sentences is often the single most effective way to raise a readability score — more effective than swapping in shorter synonyms for a few multisyllable words.

Tip: If your score is lower than you'd like, scan for your longest sentences first and look for a natural place to split them into two — this usually moves the needle more than word-by-word simplification.

How Syllable Counting Actually Works

Automated syllable counting doesn't look words up in a dictionary — it counts groups of consecutive vowels as a proxy for syllable boundaries, with some adjustments for common patterns like a silent trailing "e." This heuristic gets the vast majority of words right but isn't perfect: irregular words, especially ones borrowed from other languages or with unusual letter patterns, can be off by a syllable. Across a full paragraph or article, these small errors tend to average out and don't meaningfully shift the overall score.

Reading Ease vs. Grade Level

Flesch Reading Ease and the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level are computed from the exact same two inputs (words per sentence, syllables per word) but scaled onto different, more intuitive axes — Reading Ease runs 0–100 with higher being easier, while Grade Level maps roughly onto U.S. school grades, so a score of 8.0 suggests the text is comfortably readable by someone with an eighth-grade reading level. They'll always move in the same direction together since they're derived from identical underlying measurements; picking between them is really just a matter of which scale is more useful for your audience.

FAQ

What do the Flesch Reading Ease and Grade Level scores mean? Flesch Reading Ease runs from 0 to 100, where higher means easier to read (90+ is very easy, below 30 is very difficult). The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level instead estimates the U.S. school grade a reader would need to easily understand the text, like 8.0 meaning eighth-grade level.

How accurate is the syllable counting? It uses a standard vowel-group counting heuristic, the same approach most readability tools use, which is a close approximation rather than a perfect dictionary-based count — it can be off by one syllable on some irregular words, but averages out well across a full piece of text.

What's a good target score for general web content? Most style guides for general audiences (like newspapers) aim for a Flesch Reading Ease of roughly 60–70, corresponding to an 8th–9th grade reading level — easy enough for a broad audience without feeling oversimplified.

Is my text sent anywhere? No — the entire analysis happens locally in your browser using JavaScript, so nothing you paste in is ever sent to a server.
