Why Stop Words Skew Every Word Frequency List

Run any English text through a naive word counter without filtering, and the top of the list will almost always be the same handful of words — "the," "a," "is," "and" — regardless of what the text is actually about. That's not a bug in the counting; it's just how English grammar works, and it's exactly why frequency tools include a filter for it.

What Stop Words Are and Why They Dominate

Stop words are the small set of grammatical connective words — articles, prepositions, common pronouns, auxiliary verbs — that appear in nearly every sentence regardless of subject matter. Because they're structurally required so often, a raw frequency count of a 500-word article might show "the" appearing 30+ times while the article's actual subject word appears only 5–10 times. Filtering them out is what turns a frequency count into something that actually reflects the topic of the text, rather than just reflecting how English sentences are built.

Case and Punctuation Normalization

Before counting, a frequency tool needs to decide that "Fox," "fox," and "fox," (with trailing punctuation) are all the same word — otherwise the count would fragment across capitalization and punctuation variants and understate how often a word actually appears. This normalization step (lowercasing everything and stripping punctuation) is what makes the counts meaningful, but it does mean the tool can't distinguish a proper noun from a common noun that happens to share the same letters, like "Rose" the name versus "rose" the flower.

Tip: If you're checking your own writing for overused words, run it with stop words included at least once — an unusually high count of a specific connective word (like relying heavily on "but" or "so") can reveal a stylistic tic that's worth varying, even though it's not a topic word.

Reading the Results Correctly

A high frequency count doesn't automatically mean a word is overused in a problematic way — it might just mean the text is genuinely and appropriately focused on that topic. The signal worth paying attention to is usually a word appearing far more often than its synonyms would in natural writing, or a specific word repeating within a short span of text where a reader would actually notice the repetition, rather than the raw count across an entire long document.

Using It for SEO Without Overdoing It

For content written with search intent in mind, a word frequency count is a quick sanity check on whether your target topic and its related terms actually appear enough to signal relevance, and conversely whether any single term is repeated so often it reads unnaturally. Search engines have long since moved past simple keyword-density scoring, so the real goal here is readability and natural topic coverage, not hitting a specific percentage.
