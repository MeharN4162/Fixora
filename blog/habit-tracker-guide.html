How Streak Counting Actually Works

A streak counter looks like a simple number, but the logic behind it — and the reason it resets so unforgivingly — is worth understanding, especially if you've ever been frustrated watching a long streak disappear after one missed day.

Why Streaks Count Backward From Today

Streak calculation works by starting at today and walking backward one day at a time, checking whether each day is marked complete, and stopping the count the moment it hits a day that isn't. This backward-from-today approach is what makes a streak meaningful as "how many consecutive days including now" rather than "how many days total have I ever completed" — a habit you did diligently for 30 days two months ago and then abandoned shows a streak of 0 today, not 30, because the streak reflects your current consistency, not your history.

Why One Missed Day Resets Everything

The backward-walking logic is exactly why a single missed day breaks the whole streak rather than just subtracting one from the count — the algorithm stops counting the instant it finds a gap, so everything before that gap becomes irrelevant to the current streak number, even if you'd been consistent for months beforehand. This is a deliberate design choice common to nearly every streak-based habit app, not a harsh technical quirk — it's meant to reflect unbroken consistency specifically, which is part of what makes streaks psychologically motivating in the first place.

Tip: If you know you'll miss a day (travel, illness, a planned break), consider whether the habit genuinely needs to be daily — some habits are more sustainably tracked on a "X times per week" basis rather than a strict daily streak, which avoids the all-or-nothing reset entirely.

Retroactive Check-Ins vs. "Cheating"

Being able to mark a past day complete after the fact isn't a loophole — it's a practical acknowledgment that you don't always check in on a tracker the moment you do the actual habit. Someone who exercises in the morning but doesn't open the tracker until that evening, or forgets entirely until the next day, should still be able to log it accurately. The honesty of the streak depends on you, not on the tool enforcing same-day-only entry, which would actually make the tracker less useful for real-world habits.

Where the Data Actually Lives

A habit tracker with no account and no sign-up has to store your data somewhere, and the answer is your browser's local storage — a small storage area tied specifically to that browser on that device. It persists across visits and browser restarts, which is why your habits and streaks are still there next time you open the page, but it's also why the data won't follow you to a different browser or a different device: local storage is genuinely local, not synced anywhere.

FAQ

How is the streak calculated? It counts consecutive days, working backward from today, that are marked complete. As soon as a day is found that isn't checked off, the streak count stops there.

Can I mark a past day as complete if I forgot to check in? Yes — the last 7 days are all clickable, not just today, so you can retroactively fill in a day you missed logging.

Where is my habit data stored? In your browser's local storage on your own device — not on a server or account. It will still be there next time you visit in the same browser, but won't sync to another device.

Is my data ever sent to a server? No — everything is stored and managed entirely in your browser using JavaScript and local storage.
