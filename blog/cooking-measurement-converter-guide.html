Cooking measurement conversion looks like the simplest kind of math — until you remember that a "cup" can mean two different volumes depending on which country wrote the recipe, and that converting volume to weight isn't really a conversion at all without knowing what you're measuring.

Volume vs. Weight: Why a Cup of Flour Isn't a Cup of Sugar

A pure volume converter — teaspoons, tablespoons, cups, milliliters — works the same way a measuring cup itself works, regardless of what's inside it. But a cup of flour and a cup of granulated sugar don't weigh the same amount, because they pack differently: flour is lighter and airier, sugar is denser. Converting a volume measurement to a weight measurement like grams isn't a fixed ratio at all — it requires knowing the specific ingredient's density, which is why recipes that give weights for dry ingredients are usually more precise than ones that only give cup measurements.

US Cups vs. Metric Cups

A US customary cup is defined as exactly 236.588 milliliters. Several other countries use a slightly different "metric cup," typically defined as 250 milliliters — close enough to cause only minor differences in most recipes, but enough to matter in baking, where ratios are more sensitive. If a recipe doesn't specify which cup it means, it's worth checking the source's country of origin before assuming.

Tip: When a recipe gives a weight in grams for a dry ingredient like flour, don't reverse-convert it into cups using a volume tool — weight and volume aren't interchangeable for solids. If a scale is available, measuring the gram amount directly is far more accurate than converting back to cups.

How Oven Temperature Conversion Works

Converting Fahrenheit to Celsius uses one exact formula: °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9 (and the reverse: °F = °C × 9/5 + 32). This math is precise — there's no approximation involved. Any real-world discrepancy between a recipe's stated temperature and how your food actually bakes comes from your specific oven's calibration, not from any rounding in the conversion itself.

Teaspoons, Tablespoons, and the Math Between Them

Two ratios are worth memorizing because they come up constantly: 3 teaspoons make 1 tablespoon, and 16 tablespoons make 1 US cup. Unlike the volume-to-weight problem above, these are fixed relationships that never change regardless of what you're measuring, since they're all still just units of volume.
