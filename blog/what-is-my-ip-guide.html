Public vs. Private IP Addresses: What "My IP" Actually Means

"What's my IP" sounds like it should have one simple answer, but a single device actually has more than one IP address at once, and which one a tool reports back depends entirely on where in the network that tool is looking from.

Public vs. Private IP Addresses

Your private IP address is assigned by your router to identify your device on your own local network — it's typically something like 192.168.1.42, and it's meaningless outside your home or office network. Your public IP address is the one your router or ISP presents to the wider internet on your behalf. Every device on the same home Wi-Fi shares that one public IP even though each device has its own distinct private IP — this is why "what's my IP" tools report one shared number for an entire household, not a separate one per device.

How a Website Even Knows Your IP

Every request your browser sends across the internet has to include a return address so the response can find its way back — that return address is your public IP, visible to literally every website you visit, not just dedicated "what's my IP" tools. A page displaying your IP isn't doing anything special or invasive; it's simply showing you the address that was already attached to your own request the moment it left your network.

IPv4 vs. IPv6, and Why Both Still Exist

IPv4, the older and still more common format, is written as four numbers separated by dots (like 203.0.113.42), and it only has room for about 4.3 billion unique addresses — a number the internet has already technically exhausted, worked around mostly through address sharing techniques like NAT. IPv6 was designed to fix that shortage with a vastly larger address space, written as longer hexadecimal groups separated by colons. Because the transition has been gradual, most networks and devices today support both, and which one you're assigned can depend on your specific ISP and router configuration.

Tip: If your public IP shown here doesn't match what a different "what's my IP" site reports at the exact same moment, try both again after a few minutes rather than assuming something's broken — brief disagreements between lookup services are common and usually resolve on their own.

Why Your IP Can Change (or Look Different)

Most home internet connections use a "dynamic" IP that your ISP can reassign periodically — restarting your router is a common trigger. Using a VPN or proxy also changes what any website sees, since your traffic appears to originate from the VPN server's IP rather than your own. None of this indicates a problem; it's simply how IP addressing works when you're not paying for a fixed, "static" IP specifically.

FAQ

Where does a "what's my IP" page actually get the address from? Fixoona has no backend server of its own, so this page asks a well-known public IP-lookup service (ipify.org) directly from your browser, and that service reports back the IP address it sees your request coming from — the same way any website you visit sees your IP.

Is my public IP the same as my private home network IP? No — this is your public IP address, the one your router or ISP presents to the outside internet. If multiple devices share one internet connection (like everyone on the same home Wi-Fi), they'll typically all show the same public IP, even though each device also has its own separate private IP on the local network.

Why do IPv4 and IPv6 addresses look so different? IPv4 addresses are written as four numbers separated by dots (like 203.0.113.42) and only allow about 4.3 billion unique addresses, which the internet has essentially run out of; IPv6 uses a much longer format written in hexadecimal groups separated by colons, providing a vastly larger address space, and many networks now assign both at once.

Why might my IP look different than it did an hour ago, or different from another site's lookup? If you're using a VPN, proxy, or your ISP just rotated your assigned address, different lookup services can briefly disagree, or you might see a different result minutes apart. This is normal and reflects real, dynamic changes in how you're routed to the internet, not an error.
