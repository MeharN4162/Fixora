Runners rarely talk in miles per hour — they talk in minutes per mile. That's not just habit; pace and speed answer two different questions, and understanding which one you actually need makes race planning a lot less confusing.

Pace vs. Speed: Two Ways to Measure the Same Run

Speed is distance covered per unit of time — miles per hour or kilometers per hour. Pace is the inverse: time taken per unit of distance, like minutes per mile. Both describe the exact same run, just phrased differently. Speed answers "how far will I get in an hour," while pace answers "how long will each mile take" — and for a runner planning splits during a race, pace is almost always the more directly useful number.

Converting Between Pace and Speed

Because pace and speed are reciprocals of each other, converting between them just means flipping the ratio: divide 60 by your minutes-per-mile pace to get miles per hour, or divide 60 by your speed in mph to get minutes per mile. The same relationship holds for kilometers — divide 60 by your pace in minutes per kilometer to get kilometers per hour.

Tip: If you're pacing a race by feel, converting your goal finish time into a per-kilometer or per-mile split (rather than thinking only about the total time) makes it much easier to check whether you're on track partway through, instead of only finding out at the finish line.

Why Even Pacing Is Harder Than It Sounds

A negative split means running the second half of a race faster than the first. Many coaches consider it a strong pacing strategy, because starting slightly conservative preserves energy for the later miles, when fatigue would otherwise force a slowdown anyway. Starting too fast feels great for the first mile and almost always costs more time later than it gained early — which is exactly why a calculator based on a perfectly even pace is a planning tool, not a guarantee of how the actual race will unfold.

Predicting a Finish Time From Your Pace

Once you know your pace per mile or kilometer from a training run, multiplying it by a target race distance gives a straightforward estimate of finish time at that same, unchanging pace. It's a useful baseline for setting a goal, but real races involve terrain, weather, and fatigue that an even-pace calculation can't account for — treat the number as a starting point for a race plan, not a prediction carved in stone.

FAQ

How is pace different from speed? Speed is distance covered per unit of time (like miles per hour), while pace is the inverse — time taken per unit of distance (like minutes per mile). Runners typically think in pace since it directly answers "how long will each mile take."

Why do some races use negative splits as a strategy? A negative split means running the second half of a race faster than the first, which many coaches consider a strong pacing strategy because starting slightly conservative preserves energy for later miles when fatigue would otherwise force a slowdown. An even or negative split typically produces a better overall time than starting too fast and fading.

Can I predict my finish time for a race distance using my pace? Yes — enter your pace and the target race distance, and it multiplies your per-mile or per-kilometer pace by the total distance to estimate a finish time.

Does this account for pacing changes during a race, like fatigue? No — this assumes a perfectly even pace throughout, which is a simplification. Real race performance often varies due to terrain, fatigue, and pacing strategy, so treat the prediction as a rough estimate rather than a guarantee.
